Here are some textbooks that I think are truly wonderful and designed to be learned from, rather than just referenced by experts:

  • SILVERMAN, Hoffstein, Pipher -> An Introduction to Mathematical Cryptography.
    • This is the kind of pedagogical execellence I dream of reaching: an absolutely beautiful text which assumes essentially no knowledge of groups / fields yet builds all the way to pairing based elliptic curve cryptography, lattices, and more. Superbly well written and clear explanation of the most famous algorithms with concrete, worked examples used in exactly the right amount. Silverman is clean, readable, and simply lovely.
  • John M. HOWIE -> Fields and Galois Theory.
    • I have never seen a text which answers the "What is it?" and "Why do we care?" questions so efficiently as Howie: despite being pretty short, it's approachable (even with a self-contained ring theory introduction) and encouraging and insightful in the best ways. Not to mention: there are solutions in the back!
  • COX, Little, O'Shea -> Ideals, Varieties, and Algorithms.
    • It won the 2016 Leroy Steele prize for mathematical exposition so you know it's good: IVA provides an incredibly natural, motivated, and well-paced study of commutative algebra, algebraic geometry, and even some algebraic number theory all at the same time. The "algorithms" part of the title is what keeps everything beautifully grounded, practical, and focused: at any point you can stop and verify each theorem in Sage/Mathematica/Magma on concrete examples, of which the text has an abundance!
